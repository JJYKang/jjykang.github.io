Bells & Whistles: Gradient Orientations

I computed orientations from the smoothed DoG gradients without built-in angle functions. I reduced the derivative ratio to the first octant, approximated arctangent with a 12-term series, then restored the octant and quadrant from the derivative signs and magnitudes. Angles increase clockwise from the right because image y increases downward.

Hue shows gradient direction, saturation is one, and brightness shows magnitude scaled by its 99th percentile and clipped to [0, 1]. Flat regions are black. The gradient points toward increasing intensity, perpendicular to the edge.

Part 2: Fun with Frequencies

Part 2.1: Image Sharpening

A Gaussian retains low frequencies. Subtracting this blur from the image gives the high-frequency detail: H = I − I ∗ G. Unsharp masking adds a scaled version back: Isharp = I + αH. This increases local contrast around edges.

I combined the operation into one filter: K = (1 + α)δ − αG, where δ is an impulse at the kernel center. Each RGB channel is convolved with K using symmetric padding. All results use a 9×9 Gaussian with σ = 2; values are clipped to [0, 1] only when saving.

Taj Mahal and Sharpening Strength

The high-frequency displays below show 0.5 + 4H: gray is zero, with signed details amplified for visibility. This display scaling is not used for sharpening.

Part 2.2: Hybrid Images

A hybrid combines the low frequencies of one image with the high frequencies of another: Ihybrid = IA ∗ Glow + (IB − IB ∗ Ghigh). Fine detail dominates nearby; at a distance, the blurred image becomes more visible. View the results close up, then step back or reduce the page zoom.

I manually selected two corresponding landmarks and used them to match translation, rotation, and uniform scale. Inputs are resized to a maximum dimension of 512 pixels before alignment, then cropped to shared content. Gaussian kernels extend to three standard deviations and use symmetric padding. Filtered components are added before clipping for display.

Part 2.3: Gaussian and Laplacian Stacks

I built six-level stacks for the supplied apple and orange. Each input is resized once to 480×344 pixels; every computed stack level keeps that resolution. No pyramid functions or downsampling are used.

G0 is the original. Each subsequent Gaussian level blurs the preceding level with σ = 2, 4, 8, 16, and 32. I construct the 1D Gaussian kernels and convolve along rows and columns separately, using symmetric boundaries. This progressively removes finer frequencies without changing image size.

Each band is Li = Gi − Gi+1. The final level stores G5 as the low-pass residual, so summing all six levels reconstructs the original. Fine bands show surface texture and sharp boundaries; coarser bands show broader edges and shading.

Part 2.4: Multiresolution Blending

I reused the six-level Gaussian and Laplacian stacks from 2.3. At each level, a Gaussian-smoothed mask weights the two Laplacian bands: Bi = MiLA,i + (1 − Mi)LB,i. Summing the weighted bands and the final low-pass residual reconstructs the blend. Fine details transition narrowly; coarse color and lighting transition more gradually.

The supplied apple/orange example and its band contributions are shown in 2.3. The two custom blends below use curved, nonrectangular masks. White selects A and black selects B. Both use six levels, starting with σ = 2 and doubling the blur scale at each level.
